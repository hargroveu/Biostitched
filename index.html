Build the machine that stitches the sequins.

BioStitched is a small open-source computer numerical control machine that automates tambour embroidery. A couture hand technique used by trained embroiderers in order to embellish surfaces and textiles with different embellishments in eveningwear. This guide covers everything you need in order to build your own prototype. Give it a try. Change it. Share what you learn in the open blog.

Why build your own?

Own the tool

Embellished eveningwear fabric comes from industrial suppliers with minimum orders that small designers can’t meet. A machine you build yourself lets you make it in small batches.

Change the materials

Most prom and eveningwear embellishment is petroleum-based and hard to recycle. BioStitched is built around natural-fibre fabrics and biodegradable embellishments.

Know the real cost

Building your own tool moves cost into your own time. The guide records the hours, the failures and the fixes, so you can plan for them.

What is tambour embroidery?

Tambour embroidery is a chain stitch made with a fine hook. The fabric is stretched tight on a frame, the hook pierces it from above, catches the thread underneath and pulls a loop back up. Each new loop locks the one before it, so one continuous thread forms the whole line.

It grew out of ari work, a hooked chain stitch practised in India for centuries, and reached Europe in the 18th century, where it took the French name tambour, meaning drum, after the round frame that holds the fabric taut. In the 19th century, embroiderers in Lunéville, France, began attaching beads and sequins with the hook from the back of the fabric. Couture ateliers still work this way, which is why tambour sits behind so much beaded and sequinned eveningwear.

Test code

Five Arduino sketches, each testing one part of the machine, in the order you build it. If something doesn’t move, you’ll know exactly where to look.

Open a sketch in the Arduino IDE, choose Tools > Board > Arduino Uno and your port, then click Upload. Tests 2 and 5 need the AccelStepper library (Sketch > Include Library > Manage Libraries). Every pin number and angle is a starting point: tune them to your build.

Before you start

Do I need an engineering background?

No. The guide was written by a fashion designer who learned the electronics, 3D printing and code while building the machine, and it assumes you’re starting from the same place.

What can the machine make?

Small test swatches of chain-stitched, sequinned fabric. It’s a prototype for working out the technique, not yet a production machine.

What will it cost me?

The guide includes a fully costed parts list, plus the tools you need and the cost of the attempts that failed, so you can budget before you start. [COST SUMMARY]

What materials does it use?

Natural-fibre base fabrics and biodegradable embellishments, including sequins and beads 3D-printed in PHA. Where biodegradability is claimed but not yet tested, the guide says so.

Do I need a workshop?

A desk at home works. Prototype II is being built in a home studio with a 3D printer and a soldering kit; a makerspace or Fab Lab gives you access to the same tools.

Can I change it and share it?

Yes, that’s the point. Adapt the machine to your own practice, then share what you changed in the open blog so the next builder starts further ahead.

Made by a designer, for designers.

BioStitched is designed and built by U’lia Hargrove, a prom and eveningwear designer, as part of an MA at London College of Fashion, University of the Arts London.

It started from a practical problem: the embellished fabric used for prom and eveningwear comes from industrial suppliers with minimum orders, in synthetic materials that are hard to recycle. Building the machine is the research: what it takes, in money, skills and time, for one designer to make their own tool.

Prototype I was built in the LCF Digital Learning Lab alongside a technician. Prototype II is being built in a home studio.
